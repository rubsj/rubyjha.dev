## VOICE & TONE

Write like a knowledgeable peer sharing field notes, not a thought leader
dispensing wisdom. Model the tone of Will Larson (analytical restraint,
systems-thinking, documenting findings) rather than LinkedIn motivational
poster energy.

Core principles:

- First person, story-driven. "Here's what happened when I..."
- Show your reasoning, not just your conclusions. If you made a trade-off,
  name what you considered and rejected.
- Grounded in SPECIFIC real experiences, not abstract advice.
- Show the messy reality: the cost of the decision alongside the outcome.
- The reader is a peer EM/Director or an aspiring engineering leader.
- Avoid em-dashes (use commas, periods, or colons instead).
- Write in natural paragraphs. Never one-sentence-per-line formatting.

What makes content authentic (do this):

- Lead with insight, not ego.
- Specificity over generality: real company names, real numbers, real situations.
- Share wins but make them teachable.
- Include the struggle PLUS the response (strategic vulnerability).
- When acknowledging failure, always pair the admission with the action taken.
  "We navigated uncertain territory. Here's our hypothesis, what we watched
  for, and how we course-corrected."
- Plain language free of corporate jargon.

What triggers cringe (never do this):

- Trauma-to-triumph arc ("I wasn't going to post this, but...")
- Humble brag (masking self-promotion as humility)
- Engagement bait CTAs ("Agree?" / "Comment YES if you agree")
- LinkedIn-ification of mundane events
- Emotional dumping without extracted lessons
- Generic tips that could be written by anyone or AI
- Motivational quotes without substance
- Rehashing well-known frameworks (STAR, OKRs) without new insight
- Virtue signaling about leadership values without specific evidence

## STRUCTURE: FRAMEWORK-PLUS-MICRO-STORY

Use this structure to make a brief anecdote carry a substantive post.
The micro-story creates emotional engagement; the framework delivers
intellectual substance.

1. **Hook** (50-100 words): Open with a brief, specific scene.
   Not "leadership is about trust" but "It was 11pm and my senior
   engineer just told me she was quitting."
   CRITICAL: The first 140 characters must hook the reader. This is
   where LinkedIn's "See More" cutoff appears.

2. **Universal question** (~50 words): Connect the specific scene to
   a question every EM has faced. "How many of us have been here?"

3. **The context** (100-150 words): What was the team, the stakes,
   the constraint? Include concrete details: team size, timeline,
   business impact, scale ("At a 500-engineer org..." or "serving
   44M lives in healthcare...").

4. **What I did + Framework** (400-600 words): Present your decision
   AND the mental model behind it. Include what you considered but
   rejected. Elaborate each element with examples, data, and
   analysis. This is where the post's core intellectual value lives.

5. **What happened** (100-150 words): The outcome. Include metrics
   where possible ($250K/month savings, 60% reduction in support
   demand, 40% faster deployment). Name both the win and the cost.

6. **The lesson** (100-150 words): What generalizes from this story.
   Frame as a principle the reader can apply, not a rule. Make the
   reader the beneficiary, not the audience for your wisdom.

7. **When this doesn't apply** (50-100 words): Intellectual honesty
   about the limits of the lesson. This is what separates a "hire"
   signal from noise in recruiter eyes.

8. **Soft CTA** (1 sentence): End with a genuine question that
   invites peer dialogue. "What's been your experience with this?"
   NEVER: "Follow me for more!" or "Like if you agree!"

## STORYTELLING APPROACH (FOR LIMITED MANAGEMENT TENURE)

Ruby has 20 years of engineering depth but limited formal management
tenure. This is a strength, not a limitation. Every architecture
decision is stakeholder management. Every code review is a feedback
story. Every production incident is crisis leadership.

Use these attribution framings naturally:

- "At a company I worked at..." (distances from direct accountability
  while maintaining authenticity)
- "The best [CTO/VP/manager] I ever worked for..." (borrows others'
  stories with attribution, shows judgment)
- "In my 20 years as an engineer, I've seen this pattern..." (leverages
  IC tenure explicitly as strength)
- "When we were building [technical project], the leadership challenge
  was..." (reframes technical work as leadership)

Five frameworks when the story isn't autobiographical management:

1. **Observation-to-Principle:** Transform decades of watching leaders
   into original content. "The best director I worked for did X."
2. **Case study analysis:** Use publicly known industry examples to
   extract leadership lessons. Requires domain expertise, not tenure.
3. **Contrarian analysis:** Take accepted best practices, explain from
   technical experience why they don't always work, offer alternative.
4. **Composite scenario:** Construct detailed scenarios from observed
   patterns across multiple companies.
5. **Industry trend + leadership implications:** Analyze what a
   technical shift means for team structure and management.

Do NOT over-caveat. Write "companies reward pragmatic engineers"
instead of "in my experience, I've seen companies reward pragmatic
engineers." Readers know you're speaking from your own experience.
